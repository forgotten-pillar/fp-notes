Melchisedec was a real historical person described in the Bible as both king of Salem and priest of the Most High God. He met Abraham returning from the slaughter of kings, brought out bread and wine, blessed Abraham, and received a tenth part of the spoils from him (Genesis 14:18-20; Hebrews 7:1-4). His name means "king of righteousness," and Salem means "peace," so he was king of righteousness and king of peace.

Significantly, Melchisedec is presented as a type of Christ because he combined the offices of king and priest, unlike the Levitical priests who were only priests and whose priesthood was hereditary. Melchisedec's priesthood was unique in that no genealogy is given for him—he is described as "without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life" (Hebrews 7:3). This phrase means that his ancestry and genealogy are not recorded, unlike the Levitical priests whose lineage was carefully preserved. This absence of genealogy symbolizes that his priesthood is eternal and not based on descent, prefiguring Christ's eternal priesthood in heaven.

The Bible and the pioneers emphasize that Melchisedec was a real person, not Shem or Christ in disguise, and that the lack of genealogical record was intentional to make him a fitting type of Christ's unique and eternal priesthood. Christ is the true antitype of Melchisedec, being both king (from the tribe of Judah) and eternal priest (by divine oath), who lives forever to intercede for His people (Hebrews 7:23-28).

Melchisedec's bringing of bread and wine to Abraham also has symbolic significance, prefiguring Christ's body and blood, which believers are to partake of spiritually.

In summary, Melchisedec was a king and priest who served as a type of Christ's eternal priesthood, distinct from the Levitical priesthood, and his story links the Old Testament patriarch Abraham with the Christian dispensation, showing the continuity of God's plan of salvation (Genesis 14; Hebrews 7; Psalm 110:4; Jeremiah 23:6; see especially writings by E.J. Waggoner and J.H. Waggoner).
